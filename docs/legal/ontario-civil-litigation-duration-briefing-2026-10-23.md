**Court:** Ontario Superior Court of Justice (SCJ), checked against Small Claims Court and Federal Court

## 1. Executive answer

SIR, here is what can and cannot be stated responsibly.

**What can be stated**

1. **No official benchmark exists.** I found no published Ontario figure for average filing-to-resolution or filing-to-trial time in SCJ civil actions. Every range in the working matrix is a hypothesis.
2. **The rules set floors, not forecasts.** Fixed deadlines cover service, defence, discovery hours, the Rule 76 five-day trial cap, expert-report service (90 and 60 days before the pre-trial conference) and the five-year set-down dismissal. Elapsed time is driven mostly by the court's scheduling queue and by how ready the parties are. [S1, S5, S8, S9]
3. **Trial length has one solid data point.** For SCJ non-jury, non-family trials that produced a reported judgment between January 2014 and June 2019, the median trial was **5 sitting days** and the mean **7**. The median wait from the end of trial to judgment was **67 days** and the mean **98**. [S17]
4. **Trials are a small share of court activity.** A secondary report of the SCJ's 2024 operational data counts **3,539 proceedings tried** against **75,561 new civil proceedings** that year. That 4.7% ratio compares different cohorts. It is not a trial rate and not a settlement rate. [S18]
5. **Current practice runs longer than the reform target.** Commentary in 2026 calls "four or five years, at least" the status quo for an action that reaches trial. [S21] The Civil Rules Review proposes a target of about two years ("Trial in Two"), but that target is not law. [S13]
6. **Reform is pending, apart from some targeted amendments.** The Civil Rules Review Final Policy Report was released 2025-12-15. A joint statement on 2026-05-28 committed to staged reform built on its recommendations. [S13, S14] The three-track system is **not in force**. Targeted amendments did take effect on 2026-02-01 (venue and mandatory forms) and 2026-09-01 (O. Reg. 275/26). [S15]

**What cannot responsibly be stated**

- A province-wide average duration for any case type.
- A precise percentage of actions that settle. "Not tried" is not the same as "settled".
- That a "full trial track" exists. No such classification exists today.
- Any filing deadline, without the claim facts, the relevant dates and the governing statute.

## 3. The clocks (Analytical requirement A)

| # | Clock | Starts | Ends | Main driver |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C0 | Pre-filing | Event or discovery of claim | Claim issued | Limitation period, evidence gathering, demand letters, pre-suit notice regimes |
| C1 | Filing to disposition (any route) | Claim issued | Final disposition: default, dismissal, discontinuance, settlement, summary judgment or trial judgment | All of the below |
| C2 | Filing to settlement | Claim issued | Signed settlement | Party leverage, mediation timing, Rule 49 offers |
| C3 | Filing to trial start | Claim issued | First trial day | Readiness steps, then the **local scheduling queue** |
| C4 | Trial sitting days | First trial day | Last day of evidence and argument | Witnesses, experts, evidentiary volume |
| C5 | Trial calendar span | First trial day | Last trial day | Sittings may not run consecutively, so the span can exceed C4 |
| C6 | Trial end to judgment | End of argument | Reasons released | Judicial reserve. Median 67 days in Ontario [S17] |
| C7 | Appeal | Judgment | Appeal decision | Court of Appeal: about 13 months on average from filing to decision in 2024 (396.66 days, for appeals decided on the merits) [S25] |
| C8 | Recovery / enforcement | Judgment | Money actually collected | Whether the defendant can pay, and Rule 60 enforcement steps |

**Why the clocks are not interchangeable:** a case can settle at any point in C1, so settlement time says nothing about trial time. C4 counts days in court, not elapsed months. C3 for the same case varies by courthouse. A judgment can arrive while C8 never ends, because a winning judgment against a defendant who cannot pay recovers nothing. Treat C7 and C8 as separate projects unless they are expressly included.

## 7. Limitation periods (Analytical requirement C)

| Provision | Content | Status |
|---|---|---|
| s. 4 | "a proceeding shall not be commenced in respect of a claim after the second anniversary of the day on which the claim was discovered" | CONFIRMED LAW (indexed primary text) [S2] |
| s. 5 | Discovery is the earlier of actual knowledge of the listed elements and the day a reasonable person ought to have known. s. 5(2) presumes knowledge on the day of the act or omission unless the contrary is proved. The methodology is set out in *Clarke v. Sun Life*, 2020 ONCA 11 | CONFIRMED LAW [S2, S26] |
| s. 15 | Ultimate period: 15 years from the act or omission, regardless of discovery, subject to listed suspensions | CONFIRMED LAW (secondary); suspensions: **LEGAL VERIFICATION REQUIRED** |
| s. 16 | "No limitation period" for listed proceedings | Heading confirmed; list: **LEGAL VERIFICATION REQUIRED** |
| s. 19 | Limitation periods in other Acts that are listed in the Schedule continue to apply | Heading confirmed: **LEGAL VERIFICATION REQUIRED** |
| s. 22 | Limitation periods apply despite agreements, subject to exceptions, including business agreements | Heading confirmed: **LEGAL VERIFICATION REQUIRED** |

**Three different clocks that are easy to confuse:**
1. **The limitation period**, which is the time to *commence*.
2. **The service deadline**, which is the time to *serve* after issuing (Rule 14.08).
3. **The administrative dismissal**, which is the time to *set down* (Rule 48.14, five years).

None of them predicts how long litigation will take.

**No filing deadline is calculated in this briefing.** That requires the claim type, the dates of the act or omission and of discovery, the parties (Crown, municipal, minor or incapable), any agreement, and the governing statute.

## 10. Operational planning checklist for meetings with Ontario litigation counsel

Ranked by impact. Nothing on this list guarantees a result or a timetable.

1. **Lock the forum and route.** Small Claims ($50,000 or less), Rule 76 ($200,000 or less), ordinary procedure, Federal Court (IP), or an arbitration clause. Ask counsel to confirm the thresholds against current e-Laws.
2. **Get a limitation audit in writing.** Counsel should give the date of the act or omission, the discovery date and its evidence, the applicable statute and any exception, and the computed deadline.
3. **Ask for a split timetable.** Counsel should separate *fixed rule deadlines* from *variable waits*, and quote today's local trial-booking lead time from the trial coordinator or the Civil Practice Court.
4. **Budget by phase.** Pleadings, discovery, motions, mediation, experts, pre-trial and trial, each with low and high cases. Under Rule 76, factor in the $50,000 costs and $25,000 disbursements recovery caps. Use Rule 49 offers to settle strategically.
5. **Check evidence readiness.** Preservation, an e-discovery plan (Rule 29.1) and witness availability.
6. **Experts.** Retain early. Back-schedule the 90-day and 60-day deadlines from the expected pre-trial date. Confirm use of the revised Form 53 after 2026-09-01.
7. **Settlement windows.** Mediation (mandatory in Toronto, Ottawa and Essex within 180 days of the first defence), the pre-trial conference, and Rule 49 offers. In the 2024 data, 43% of tracked mediation events fully settled (1,767 of 4,144: secondary report [S18]).
8. **Cost every motion.** Each one adds preparation, a hearing wait and a decision period. Consider summary judgment where the record allows (*Hryniak v. Mauldin*, 2014 SCC 7 [S27]).
9. **Get a trial estimate.** Number of witnesses, sitting days, a jury or not (none under Rule 76), and whether sittings will run consecutively.
10. **Diary the Rule 48.14 date**, which is the fifth anniversary of commencement.
11. **Monitor the reforms.** Watch the Civil Rules Review implementation notices. Advance notice of approved amendments has been promised [S14].
12. **Check collectability before suing.** A judgment does not guarantee recovery. Treat enforcement and any appeal as separate budgets.

**Blind spots you did not ask about:** adverse-costs exposure if you lose; whether pre-judgment interest accrues; jury notices outside Rule 76 (they lengthen trials and scheduling); and the Ontario Courts Public Portal transition, which is reported to have slowed some routine filings (date of report not captured).
