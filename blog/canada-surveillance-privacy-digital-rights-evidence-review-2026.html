Canada: Surveillance, Privacy and Digital Rights — Evidence Review

By Desmond Otieno Odhiambo · ClearGlass Inc. · October 27, 2026 · Public-source assessment

Five developments intersect at the boundaries of government access, public accountability and personal-data security: the proposed Lawful Access Act, automated licence-plate reading, access-to-information changes, proposed online-safety regulation and the Crime Stoppers data breach.

Assessment boundary — October 27, 2026.

This report distinguishes VERIFIED facts supported by legislation or official notices, REPORTED / ATTRIBUTED statements from identifiable reporting or stakeholders, and UNRESOLVED questions about implementation or legal effects. Proposed legislation is not described as law already in force. Legislative records, counts and investigations may change after this assessment date.

1. Confirmed findings

VERIFIED · BILL C-22

Completed second reading in the Senate on October 22 and referred to the Standing Senate Committee on National Security, Defence and Veterans Affairs. No Royal Assent as of the assessment. Parliamentary record.

VERIFIED · PRIVACY INVESTIGATION

On October 27, the federal Privacy Commissioner announced an investigation into Navigate360's safeguards related to Crime Stoppers tip data. No finding of wrongdoing was announced. Official announcement.

VERIFIED · ONTARIO ACCESS RULES

Ontario's Bill 97 changed access-to-information provisions, including exclusions affecting certain political-office records and a longer ordinary response period of 45 business days from July 1. Ontario IPC updates.

These are documentary findings, not a conclusion that all proposed surveillance capabilities are operating or that an investigation has established liability.

3. Bill C-22: authority is not deployment

On October 22, 2026, Bill C-22 moved into Senate committee consideration. The parliamentary stage matters because the legal text remains amendable and implementation would follow legislative passage and commencement provisions. Part 1's 180-day commencement language is a future conditional timeline, not evidence that those powers are already being used under the bill.

The technical questions extend beyond the bill's title: which providers could be designated; what data categories any regulations could require; whether specific obligations would affect encryption design; what oversight is available for confidential orders; and what Senate amendments might change. The government emphasizes safeguards, while providers and civil-liberties advocates have described possible security and transparency risks. These competing interpretations should be attributed until the legal and operational details are settled.

4. ALPR mapping: visibility versus network claims

Camera-location maps provide an important form of public visibility, but location data alone does not establish who operates every device, what information it records, how long records are held, or where queries can be shared. A camera pin is not proof of a unified nationwide plate-tracking database.

Meaningful local verification requires police-board and municipal records, privacy impact assessments, procurement contracts, retention schedules and access-sharing policies. Volunteer map totals should always be identified as non-exhaustive observations, not official installed-equipment totals.

5. Access to information: the audit channel

Ontario's 2026 changes and Alberta's 2025 access regime affect the systems by which the public requests records. The same rules can matter when examining camera purchases, data-sharing contracts or public-sector security practices. Yet an exemption's existence does not prove that a specific surveillance-procurement record has been withheld: that claim needs an identifiable request, refusal, commissioner decision or court record.

The relevant policy disagreement is between administrative flexibility and confidentiality on one side, and the accessibility and timeliness of public records on the other. Assess individual provisions and documented outcomes rather than assuming uniform effects across provinces.

6. Bill C-34: child safety and age assurance

Bill C-34 proposes a regulatory framework for designated online services, including certain chatbot and social-media services. Its under-16 account restrictions and future age-assurance rules raise questions about how services would verify eligibility without collecting more sensitive information than necessary. The bill was still in the House; proposed obligations should not be represented as current universal platform requirements.

Assessments should separate the government's stated objectives of reducing harms to children from implementation questions, including privacy-preserving age assurance, accessibility, expression rights, regulatory scope, and the practical effect of exceptions.

7. Crime Stoppers: what the breach proves

The Navigate360 incident illustrates the potential consequences of entrusting sensitive submissions to a third-party technology provider. CBC's analysis, widely reported on October 21, identified more than 1.5 million Canadian tips among compromised records. The federal Privacy Commissioner announced an investigation on October 27 under the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA).

Importantly, the existence of a name or address in a compromised tip does not identify that person as the submitter: submissions may refer to witnesses, victims, suspects, or other individuals. The total number of records must not be confused with a total number of affected people. A privacy investigation is not itself a finding that the vendor breached the statute.

Police-service pauses are separately verifiable operational responses. Whether and when particular programs safely resume depends on their own announcements, not on a presumed nationwide status.

8. Three interpretation safeguards

  1. A proposed retention power is not existing blanket retention. Bill C-22's regulatory architecture and any eventual implementation must be distinguished.
  2. A mapped camera is not evidence of a connected national system. Ownership, sharing, retention and capability need independent documentation.
  3. A compromised tip is not synonymous with an identified tipster. Exposure categories and the count of unique affected individuals remain separate questions.

9. What to monitor next

  • Parliament: C-22 committee witnesses, proposed amendments and the final statutory text; C-34 referral, amendments and age-assurance provisions.
  • Privacy regulators: the Navigate360 investigation, enforcement findings if published, and further breach notifications.
  • Police and municipalities: ALPR procurement decisions, approved retention schedules and any documented data-sharing arrangements.
  • Information access: concrete FOI denials, commissioner orders and changes to the federal access-review process.
