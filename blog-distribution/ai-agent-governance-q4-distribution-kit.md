## Asset 1 — LinkedIn long-form post

**Draft**

An AI agent is not just a chatbot with a longer prompt.

Once it can read files, call APIs, update records, trigger workflows, or act on behalf of a team, it becomes an operational identity that needs governance.

Before granting an agent more autonomy, answer five questions:

1. **Who owns it?** Name the accountable person and define the agent’s business purpose.
2. **What may it do?** Scope tools, data access, and permissions to the task—not the other way around.
3. **Which actions require review?** Set approval gates for sensitive, external, financial, or difficult-to-reverse actions.
4. **Can you reconstruct what happened?** Keep access-controlled logs of actions, relevant inputs, approvals, and outcomes, with an appropriate retention policy.
5. **How do you stop it?** Define credential expiry, revocation, incident escalation, and a tested shutdown path.

A useful design rule: the more consequential or irreversible an action is, the less it should rely on an agent’s own interpretation as its only control.

Governance does not have to mean removing automation. It means making the boundaries explicit before expanding autonomy.

## Asset 2 — Three short LinkedIn variants

### Variant A: Ownership

An AI agent with production access needs more than a model name.

Document the human owner, business purpose, permitted tools, data boundaries, expiry date, and revocation route. Revisit that record when the workflow changes.

If no one can answer “Who owns this agent?”, you have an ownership problem before you have a model problem.

The ClearGlass governance playbook: https://www.clearglassinc.com/blog/ai-agent-governance-governed-autonomy.html

### Variant B: Approval gates

“Human in the loop” is not a complete control description.

Which person reviews the action? What evidence do they see? What actions are blocked before approval? Is the approval recorded? What happens if the reviewer declines or is unavailable?

Define the decision boundary before the workflow is automated—not after the first unexpected result.

Practical guide: https://www.clearglassinc.com/blog/ai-agent-governance-governed-autonomy.html

### Variant C: Offboarding

AI agent offboarding deserves a real checklist.

- Disable the workflow or identity.
- Revoke associated tokens and credentials.
- Remove scheduled jobs and delegated permissions.
- Confirm retained data and logs follow policy.
- Record the owner and closure evidence.

A pilot ending is not proof that its access ended. Verify the access path.

More on governed autonomy: https://www.clearglassinc.com/blog/ai-agent-governance-governed-autonomy.html

## Asset 4 — X / Threads thread (6 posts)

**1/6** An AI agent that can call tools, update records, or trigger workflows is an operational identity—not just a chat window.

Treat its permissions and actions as part of your security architecture.

**2/6** Start with ownership: a named human owner, documented purpose, permitted tools, expiry date, and a way to revoke access.

No undocumented “temporary” agent should quietly become permanent.

**3/6** Define the action boundary. Reading approved information is not the same risk as changing production data, sending an external message, or moving money.

Different actions need different controls.

**4/6** “Human in the loop” only helps when the gate is specific: who approves, what they see, which actions are blocked, and where the decision is recorded.

**5/6** Logging and shutdown are design requirements too. Decide how you will reconstruct an action, revoke credentials, disable a workflow, and respond when an agent behaves unexpectedly.

**6/6** The useful question is not “How autonomous can we make it?” It is “What autonomy is appropriate for this action, and what evidence proves the controls worked?”

## Asset 6 — Community response template (Reddit / technical forums)

**Use only when a relevant question genuinely calls for this guidance; rewrite to answer the thread, and follow that community’s rules.**

For an AI agent that can take actions, I’d start with an inventory before adding monitoring: owner, purpose, tools, data scopes, credentials, expiry, and the actions that require a second person to approve. Then test revocation and work out what evidence you need to investigate a bad or unexpected action.

A useful follow-up is to classify actions by consequence: read-only, reversible internal write, external communication, and high-impact or hard-to-reverse operations. The control should match the action.
